Vector Rally

You do not steer the car. You steer its acceleration.

  1. Your car has a speed. Each turn you change it by one — up, down or neither, in each direction.
  2. The car then moves by its whole new speed. Working out where that lands is the game.
  3. Leave the track and you lose your speed and your turn. One lap, first one home wins.

Put it on the home screen

Android, in Chrome: the ⋮ menu, top right → Add to Home screen (it may say Install app).

iPhone and iPad, in Safari: the share button in the toolbar — the square with an arrow coming out of the top — then scroll down the share sheet and pick Add to Home Screen. It is not in Safari’s own menu, and you will not see it until you have scrolled.

Load the game once with a network connection. After that it works with no network at all.

How it works
  • Your car stands on a corner of the paper, where two lines cross. It has a position and a velocity, both pairs of whole numbers.
  • Each turn you change the velocity by −1, 0 or +1 in each direction — nine choices — and the car then travels in a straight line by the whole new velocity. Neither part of it may leave −5…5.
  • Work out where that puts you. The game will not show you: adding the velocity to the position is the part worth practising. The pad tells you what the velocity becomes, and nothing else.
  • The whole line from where you are to where you land has to stay on the track. At speed 5 that line is five units long, and it can cut the corner out of a bend and back in again with both of its ends still on the track. That counts as leaving the track, because it is.
  • Go off and the car is put back on the last whole-number point it reached with its speed set to zero, which costs you the turn. The game shows you exactly where the line left the track before it moves the car back.
  • Cars are points. Two may not stand on the same point, and a move whose line goes exactly through another car is impossible — but passing close is not.
  • A move that cannot happen at all is crossed out and cannot be taken.
  • One lap. It counts when you cross the line the way the arrows point, having passed every hidden checkpoint in order along the way. You cannot see the checkpoints; following the arrows round is enough to collect them.

The computer drivers are two different ideas rather than two levels. Greedy looks one move ahead and takes whichever gets it nearest the next checkpoint fastest, measuring in a straight line — so it accelerates towards corners it cannot see and falls off them. Planner searches every state a car could be in, several moves deep, and brakes before the corner because the search has already been round it. Both steer by checkpoints you cannot see.

The edge of the track is a curve that pays the squares no attention. It is drawn as a thin line exactly where the mathematics puts it, so what you see is what the game tests against.

Taking a turn: press one of the nine buttons and let go to take that move. Sliding off the button before letting go cancels it. Drag the track to move around it, pinch or scroll to zoom.

Install it: on Android, Chrome’s ⋮ menu → “Add to Home screen”. On iOS, the share button in Safari’s toolbar → scroll down → “Add to Home Screen”. Load the game once with a network connection and it works offline afterwards.

What is going on here
  • It keeps a number for every (point, speed) it has been in and each of the nine moves, and nudges those numbers towards what actually happened: −1 a move, −25 for leaving the track, 0 for arriving.
  • Every attempt starts somewhere random — any point on the track, any speed — rather than on the grid. Dropped on the start line every time, a car moving at random would essentially never arrive anywhere, and the curve would be a flat line along the top of the chart.
  • What it is aiming at is the next gate, not the finish line. A whole lap is further off than the discount can see: at 0.95 anything beyond about twenty moves is worth as much as anything else, the table comes out flat, and the car sits still because every move looks the same. Driving to the next gate, over and over, is a lap.
  • Nothing is saved. Reload the page and this is gone. The curve is the demonstration, and a table that was already there when the lesson began would take the demonstration away.
  • It only knows this track. Train it again for another one.
  • Other cars are not in what it remembers at all. When its favourite move is blocked it takes the best of the ones it may make, which is an honest limit of the state it was given rather than something to hide.
  • Expect it to lose to the Planner. The track never changes and can be seen in full, so searching works out exactly what learning can only approximate. That a driver with a hundred thousand attempts behind it loses to one that simply calculates is the more useful thing to know.
